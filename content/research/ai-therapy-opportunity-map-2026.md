## The short answer

The market has moved beyond standalone mental health chatbots. The strongest companies are assembling a continuous system: remembered context, conversational support, assessment, routing, clinician care, documentation, and outcome measurement. At the same time, general assistants now have vastly greater distribution, increasingly natural voice, durable memory, and health context.

That makes another generic "AI therapist" a weak position for Oceanheart. Two related practitioner-side openings survive this scan. The first is **situational recall**: helping a clinician recover one relevant source, episode, contradiction, question, or cycle at the moment it could be useful without pulling them out of listening. The second is a **shared experience layer**: helping therapist and client make a perspective visible, rehearse a response, generate a metaphor, or manipulate a provisional representation together. In both, the therapist remains the accountable clinician. The AI supplies evidence, possibilities, or material, not an autonomous relationship or diagnosis.

This is an **inference from the market map**, not a claim that no competitor is working on it. Upheal, Blueprint, and smaller formulation tools already occupy much of the broad clinician-copilot idea. The narrower research questions are whether a sparse live cue can improve recall without degrading presence, and whether a private clinician object can cross into a client-visible canvas without losing provenance or confusing authority.

## How to read the evidence

This scan covers material developments from January 2024 through 25 August 2026. Factual claims use company publications, public-company filings, institutional research pages, peer-reviewed papers, or direct X pages. Secondary reporting was used only to discover leads and is not cited.

Three parallel research tracks covered the market and business models, the HCI and research frontier, and UK clinical, privacy, and regulatory feasibility. The synthesis then tested Kai's clinician-cockpit hypothesis against the shared therapist-client experience layer.

Labels used below:

- **Verified fact:** a public filing, completed transaction record, peer-reviewed paper, or directly observable product event.
- **Company claim:** a number or outcome reported by the vendor itself and not independently verified in this scan.
- **Inference:** interpretation across sources. It is a proposition to test, not a market fact.

Scale measures are not interchangeable. "Lives eligible" describes distribution, not use. Messages and minutes describe activity, not benefit. Paying users and revenue describe a business, not clinical effectiveness.

## What materially changed in the last two years

### 1. Memory became product infrastructure

ChatGPT moved from saved facts to cross-chat history in 2025. Spring's Guide presents persistent clinical context as its core value. Rosebud treats longitudinal pattern recognition as the product. Sesame gives separate remembered context to each voice character. The interface is no longer only a good reply. It is a relationship with an accumulating model of the person.

The unresolved design question is not simply "Can it remember?" It is: who owns the remembered formulation, who can inspect and correct it, what crosses into a clinical record, and when should the system deliberately forget?

### 2. Voice crossed from feature to interaction model

Hume's EVI 2 offered subsecond voice-to-voice responses, tone sensing, and controllable personality in September 2024. Sesame's 2025 preview centred timing, interruption, prosody, and "voice presence"; its 2026 mobile preview combined voice with search, notes, text, and memory. Meta similarly made full-duplex voice central to its standalone assistant.

For therapy-adjacent work, this matters because timing, hesitation, felt presence, and turn-taking change the intervention. It also increases attachment, privacy, consent, and false-anthropomorphism risk. Naturalness is an interface capability, not proof of understanding or care.

### 3. Mental-health products became care routers

Headspace, Spring, Lyra, Wysa, and Limbic are all moving toward a layered pathway. Conversation is used to understand a need, recommend content, prepare an assessment, support work between sessions, and route to a human level of care. The commercially valuable object is the pathway and provider network, not the chatbot alone.

### 4. General assistants became the default competition

ChatGPT, Gemini, and Meta AI each operate at a distribution scale that specialist apps cannot match. ChatGPT now has health connections, memory, apps, voice, and explicit mental-health safety work. In Anthropic's analysis of 4.5 million Claude conversations, affective use was only 2.9%, but that still included coaching, counselling, relationship advice, and companionship. Anthropic cautioned that its analysis did not measure real-world outcomes or dependency. [Source](https://www.anthropic.com/news/how-people-use-claude-for-support-advice-and-companionship).

### 5. Safety moved into the product roadmap

OpenAI added emotional reliance, psychosis or mania, and self-harm to baseline safety evaluation, reporting internal improvement after work with more than 170 mental-health experts. Character.AI began removing open-ended chat for minors. Specialist providers increasingly publish model-evaluation frameworks and human-oversight rules. These are responses to visible failure modes, not evidence that the problems are finished.

### 6. Consolidation joined AI as the growth strategy

Spring closed its Alma acquisition; Teladoc acquired UpLift to bring insurance coverage into BetterHelp; Headspace acquired Supertalk for voice and memory; UHS agreed to acquire Talkspace. Distribution, reimbursement, clinical networks, and longitudinal data are being assembled together. A standalone feature can be copied or bought. A trusted workflow and relationship with practitioners is harder to reproduce.

## What the research evidence does and does not establish

The strongest recent results justify careful experimentation, but not the claim that a public generative chatbot is equivalent to therapy.

- **Therabot:** a 2025 randomized trial enrolled 210 adults and found symptom improvements against a wait-list control. Active suicidality, mania, and psychosis were excluded; a separate crisis classifier and clinician review were part of the research system. The result supports a heavily governed intervention, not autonomous public deployment or equivalence to a human therapist. [Paper](https://doi.org/10.1056/AIoa2400802), [Dartmouth account](https://ai.dartmouth.edu/news/2025/03/first-therapy-chatbot-trial-yields-mental-health-benefits).
- **Limbic:** a 2025 active-control trial with 540 adults compared its conversational app with workbooks containing the same psychoeducational material. People opened the app more often and spent longer with it, but there was no significant overall difference in anxiety or depression symptom reduction. This is stronger evidence for engagement than for a unique clinical effect. [Communications Medicine](https://www.nature.com/articles/s43856-025-01321-8).
- **Generative Woebot:** a two-week exploratory trial with 160 adults found a generative version and a rules-based version produced similar relationship ratings, while the generative version classified expressed empathy more often. The study was not powered for between-group clinical differences, excluded suicidal intent, and had no longer follow-up. [JMIR](https://www.jmir.org/2025/1/e67365/).
- **Safety:** a peer-reviewed 2025 evaluation found that general-purpose language models could stigmatise some conditions, reinforce delusional beliefs, and respond unreliably to crisis scenarios. Its practical conclusion favours augmentation with accountable human involvement. [FAccT 2025](https://doi.org/10.1145/3715275.3732039).

The decision-level reading is narrower than either hype or dismissal. Conversational systems can increase access, immediacy, disclosure, and engagement. Some controlled systems have produced promising clinical results. Safety evidence, active-control evidence, excluded populations, and short follow-up still leave autonomy, durability, adverse events, and real-world crisis use unresolved.

## The HCI frontier: move beyond the chatbot

The most useful research does not ask only whether the model gives a good answer. It asks how the interface distributes agency between person, practitioner, and system.

### Non-linear reflection

[ExploreSelf](https://younghokim.net/files/papers/exploreself-chi25.pdf), a CHI 2025 system, was designed around a problem with turn-taking chat: each reply pulls attention toward the model's last message and makes it harder for a person to see the whole conversation or change direction. Its interface let people expand themes, choose Socratic questions, toggle suggestions, and revisit earlier material. In a small 19-person lab study, perceived agency improved. The sample is exploratory, but the design point is directly relevant: therapy-adjacent reflection may work better as a navigable space than a persuasive dialogue.

### Components before whole treatments

A 2024 CHI framework for [modular mental-health intervention design](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11105670/) separates a capability, a component, a complete intervention system, and its implementation. That distinction is useful for Oceanheart. A solo builder can design and test one interaction that supports a particular experience without claiming to have built or validated a treatment programme.

### Co-creative material

Early systems are exploring AI as material rather than authority. [MindfulDiary](https://doi.org/10.1145/3613904.3642937) combined state-sensitive journalling with a clinician view in a small four-week study involving people with depression and psychiatrists. [DeepThInk](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S1071581923001489) explored AI-assisted digital art with registered art therapists. [TherAIssist](https://arxiv.org/abs/2502.12443), a preprint, combined a client canvas, therapist-configured homework agents, and compiled history in a small field study. [TheraTrack](https://arxiv.org/abs/2601.18179), also a preprint, reports a 14-therapist pilot in which clinicians used private analysis and in-session views differently and valued links from summaries back to original entries. None establishes a ready clinical product. Together they point toward traceable views, interactive objects, therapist authoring, and client navigation rather than another human-like bot.

### Design implication

The open territory is a **shared third thing in the room**: an object that therapist and client can examine, alter, reject, and use. The model generates possibilities. The client owns meaning. The therapist owns timing, containment, and clinical judgment.

## Business-model patterns

1. **Direct consumer subscription:** BetterHelp is the clearest disclosed example. Its 2025 numbers also show the constraint: $518.5 million of advertising and marketing against $950.4 million revenue while users declined. The inferred lesson is that broad consumer acquisition can consume the advantage created by cheap AI.

2. **Employer or health-plan access:** Spring, Lyra, Headspace, Wysa, and Limbic sell distribution to organisations. Pricing is commonly based on eligible populations, per-member access, services, or outcomes. The metric to demand is active use and validated outcome by cohort, not only "lives covered".

3. **Insurance-reimbursed clinical sessions:** Talkspace, BetterHelp through UpLift, Alma, and Headspace Therapy bind software to licensed provider networks. This creates a stronger revenue route than a wellness subscription but adds credentialing, jurisdiction, integration, and care-delivery complexity.

4. **Clinician SaaS:** Eleos and Upheal sell time, compliance, documentation, supervision, and EHR workflow. The buyer can calculate minutes saved, notes completed, or audit exposure. This is a crowded category, and session capture creates a high consent and data-governance burden.

5. **Companion engagement:** Character.AI and products such as Nomi monetise recurring conversational relationships. The core product metric is continued engagement. In a mental-health context, that incentive can conflict with autonomy, real-world relationships, and deliberate endings. This is an inferred structural tension, not a claim of harm by every companion.

6. **Model, API, or platform licensing:** Hume sells voice infrastructure; OpenAI embeds third-party apps; Slingshot is pursuing a psychology model for providers and direct use. This can reach many products without owning care delivery, but model capability is easy for larger platforms to bundle.

7. **Consolidated care platform:** The acquisitions around Spring, BetterHelp, Headspace, and Talkspace show a move toward owning navigation, provider capacity, reimbursement, data, and AI together. This is where the largest specialist players appear to be heading.

## The clearest gaps

### 1. Live, therapist-led AI experiences

Most visible products either speak privately to the user or work behind the clinician. There is room for an interface explicitly designed for three participants: person, practitioner, and model. Examples include visualising competing stories, generating a metaphor together, interviewing a protective part, comparing predictions with outcomes, rehearsing a difficult exchange, or manipulating a thought as an object on screen.

The design advantage is not better automated empathy. It is giving the practitioner fast, surprising material while keeping responsibility, timing, and interpretation human.

### 2. A bounded authoring studio for practitioners

Therapists need a way to create small exercises without pretending to train a therapist model. A useful system would let a practitioner define purpose, contraindications, language, stopping conditions, allowed inputs, retention, and what the client sees. It would include synthetic scenario testing and a clear preview of every instruction sent to the model.

This looks more feasible and defensible for a solo practitioner-builder than a general companion. The practitioner's method and judgment become the product surface.

### 3. Inspectable, patient-controlled continuity

Vendors increasingly sell memory, but their public descriptions rarely make the formulation itself a first-class object that a person can inspect, correct, selectively share, or expire. A portable "what I am practising and why" record could connect a live session, life between sessions, and the next conversation without becoming a hidden clinical dossier.

### 4. Multimodal defusion and perspective change

Voice is advancing quickly, but the market still leans on conversation. There is space for visual, spatial, playful, and embodied interfaces: maps of viewpoints, movable thought objects, generated scenes, future-self dialogues, sound and pacing shifts, or short mixed-media exercises. The clinical question is whether a chosen interaction helps this person notice and move, not whether the model sounds human.

### 5. Evaluation at the level of the moment

The market reports lives, messages, minutes, notes, satisfaction, and symptom scales. There is less public evidence about the mechanism of a single AI-supported moment: did the person gain distance from a thought, see another option, act in line with a value, or communicate differently? A small product can compete by measuring a narrow intended change honestly and publishing failures as well as wins.

Keep three outcome horizons separate. Immediate articulation, perceived distance, or novelty measures the interaction. Later action or recall measures whether anything carried into life. Adverse effects, unwanted dependence, or displacement of human contact need their own follow-up. A striking in-session moment is not evidence of durable cognitive or behavioural change.

### 6. Relationship boundaries as a feature

Memory and natural voice make attachment easier. A wellbeing product can differentiate by designing for exits, real-world action, human contact, and explicit limits. It should be able to say, in effect, "this exercise is complete; take the next step away from the screen." That is the opposite of an engagement-maximising companion.

### 7. Independent evaluation and implementation services

Many providers can buy a model. Fewer can specify safe interaction behaviour, test it across difficult conversations, map consent and data flow, or run a synthetic-first pilot with clinicians. A solo consultant-practitioner can plausibly sell evaluation, facilitation design, workshops, and prototype studies before building a regulated product.

## Primary hypothesis 1: the private Clinician Cockpit

Kai's primary hypothesis is clinician-side augmentation during remote therapy. The client continues to interact only with the human clinician. The core problem is **situational recall at the moment of usefulness**: an experienced clinician can miss a high-value moment because the relevant prior episode, contradiction, formulation, question, or reinforcement cycle is not in working memory when it matters.

The aim is not to replace knowledge or judgment. It is to make the right source or possibility available while that judgment is active.

### Two complementary clinician-side modes

#### Slow workspace outside the session

This is a high-bandwidth private space for deliberate thinking when attention is not time-bound. It can show:

- source-linked timelines and connected episodes;
- changes, contradictions, and unresolved questions;
- alternative provisional formulations;
- relationship graphs and reinforcement cycles;
- candidate questions with supporting and contradicting evidence.

The clinician can expand sources, compare interpretations, edit a graph, ask for counterevidence, and decide what should remain available later. Density is acceptable because this is preparation and reflection, not live listening.

#### Fast cue rail inside the session

This is not a small version of the workspace. It is a separate interaction model with an extreme attention budget:

- one sparse cue at a time;
- the source or source IDs visible immediately;
- retrieved evidence visually distinct from model inference;
- one-key dismissal and no penalty for ignoring it;
- no modal dialogs, scrolling feed, animated urgency, or demand for a response;
- no automatic promotion into memory or the clinical record;
- an instant pause that leaves the session unaffected.

A cue might say `CLIENT | VERBATIM | CURRENT: In session 4, the client linked Sunday evenings with withdrawal` or `MODEL | HYPOTHESIS | SOURCE-LINKED: Possible question: what changed between the two episodes?` It should not say what the pattern means or what the clinician must do.

The proposed cockpit therefore has two deliberately different time horizons:

- A **bounded longitudinal memory** containing source-linked episodes and explicitly retained working material.
- A **disposable live renderer** showing possible questions, timelines, provisional formulations, relationship graphs, or reinforcement cycles for the current session.

Detailed timelines, graphs, and formulations belong in the slow workspace. The live rail may surface a compact reference to one of them, but it should not turn the session into dashboard operation.

### Navigational naming

“Waypoint is the quiet in-session cue, a navigational point to steer by right now. Wake Log is the longitudinal record, the trace of where the journey has actually gone over time, patterns you can look back at. Throughline is the coherent story that ties those points together without pretending it's the final truth.”

#### The watchkeeping family

A watch is a bounded period of duty. Each half hour was marked by the ship's bell, and eight bells marked the end of the watch. Historically, another member of the watch might turn the half-hour glass and strike the bell rather than the Officer of the Watch doing it personally. [Royal Museums Greenwich](https://www.rmg.co.uk/cutty-sark/attractions/cutty-sark-ships-bell) and the [US Naval History and Heritage Command](https://www.history.navy.mil/content/history/nhhc/browse-by-topic/heritage/customs-and-traditions0/the-ship-s-bell.html) document this timekeeping practice.

During that bounded period, the Officer of the Watch maintains the situational picture, checks position and conditions, records notable events, and hands responsibility to the relieving officer. The [UK Officer of the Watch occupational standard](https://occupational-maps.skillsengland.education.gov.uk/maps/occupation/OCC0842) describes the watch, record, and handover duties.

The session mapping is:

- **The Watch:** the bounded session and its live monitor.
- **The Bell:** timed signals as the session approaches closure.
- **Regular fixes:** brief checks of time, speaking balance, and session direction.
- **Handover:** deliberate integration and ending.

The practitioner remains the Officer of the Watch. The software keeps the glass, rings the bell, and shows the readings. It does not decide what the readings mean or what action to take.

The provisional workspace split is:

- **The Bridge:** the live in-session workspace containing Waypoint, Body Weather, and The Watch.
- **Chartroom:** the slower preparation and reflection workspace containing Wake Log, Throughline, Captain's Log, and Second Bearing.

"Clinician's Cockpit" remains a working moniker, not a settled product name. The wider umbrella remains unnamed while the Bridge and Chartroom distinction is tested.

#### Octopus topology note

The operator's working metaphor is an octopus: partly autonomous tentacle-level processing connected through a ring-like nervous system to a larger coordinating unit. Applied here, each instrument could have bounded local autonomy while a coordinating layer governs what may share context or reach the clinician's attention. This is a design image, not an accepted architecture. The first test remains a small provider-neutral instrument with an explicit coordination contract.

#### Lab repository principle

> One lab, separate benches, no heavy plumbing.

This is the opening rule for the future private lab repository. Each instrument should be an independently runnable application with its own dependencies, run command, tests, and owned output area. The Bridge and Chartroom compose the views but do not own instrument logic or state. Shared code, plugin machinery, a central model router, and separate repositories must earn their place through repeated need. Until then, the lab shares only synthetic fixtures and a small data contract.

Cloud inference may currently offer the best latency and capability for this interaction. That is a technical hypothesis to benchmark on the actual tasks, not a governance conclusion. An informed client choice does not by itself settle data-protection law, confidentiality, medical-device status, security, or professional responsibility.

### The market gap is narrower than it first appears

[Upheal's AI Assistant](https://www.upheal.io/support/en/articles/14704718-upheal-ai-assistant) is a direct comparator. Its 2026 beta can search a client's notes and transcripts, surface themes and possible blind spots, suggest approaches grounded in history, and link claims back to sources. [Upheal says it is available during its telehealth sessions](https://feedback.upheal.io/changelog/meet-ai-assistant-secure-hipaa-compliant-ai-chat). Assistant conversations are deleted after seven days of inactivity, while its separate memory can retain practitioner preferences. [Upheal's technical account](https://www.upheal.io/support/en/articles/15261899-how-the-ai-assistant-works) says record-based and clinical claims include source links and that clinical input is framed as suggestion rather than decision.

[Blueprint](https://www.blueprint.ai/session-prep) creates pre-session briefs from prior documentation, including themes, stuck points, progress, possible focus areas, and opening prompts. [Mentalyc](https://www.mentalyc.com/ai-progress-tracker) extracts and plots longitudinal symptom, goal, and alliance signals. [NovoPsych](https://novopsych.com/) markets editable genograms and visual formulation diagrams generated from notes or transcripts. [Aetios](https://www.aetios-ai.com/) markets EHR-derived formulations and time-based node networks. Public maturity and independent validation vary, but formulation graphics and longitudinal clinical synthesis are not empty categories.

[Lyssn](https://www.lyssn.io/our-solutions/quality-improvement/) markets real-time session feedback, transcript search, topic coding, and fidelity measures. [Eleos](https://eleos.health/individual-therapy/) captures live session material for notes and surfaces session-specific insights and follow-up suggestions. These are company descriptions, not independent proof of value or safety.

The remaining opening is therefore not "AI remembers my client and gives me ideas". Several products already approach that. The narrower possible opening is **situational recall with a very small attention footprint**: a slow evidence workspace feeding one inspectable, disposable cue at the moment it becomes useful. The public product surfaces reviewed here did not establish that full interaction contract, especially strict evidence-versus-inference separation, sparse live cues, measured attention cost, and item-level retention. That is a gap hypothesis to validate with practitioners, not a claim of empty territory.

### The hidden influence hazard

The private screen reduces the risk that a client mistakes the model for a therapist. It creates another risk: the client cannot see or correct the material shaping the clinician's attention.

A weak suggestion can change the next question, reinforce an early formulation, pull attention away from a live cue, or make a retrieved episode seem more relevant than it is. The clinician remains the speaker and decision-maker, but the system can still mediate care. A generic "human in the loop" label does not address anchoring, selective retrieval, automation bias, or a clinician's limited capacity to verify suggestions while maintaining attunement.

The product therefore has to make influence inspectable at the moment it occurs. A model suggestion should become an open question to test with the client, never a fact about the client. If the system materially shapes a clinical decision, the clinician remains responsible for deciding what belongs in the approved clinical record and why.

The HCI tension is direct: the system must improve recall without consuming the attention needed to listen. More cues are not better. The relevant product metric is useful recall per interruption, with false salience, gaze time, missed client content, and uncritical adoption counted as costs.

### Inspectable cockpit contract

| Lane | What it contains | Required provenance | Default persistence |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Recorded source material** | Exact client statement, document excerpt, or other approved source with enough surrounding context | Source owner, stable source ID, date, session or document, and retrieval query | Ephemeral unless the clinician pins the citation |
| **Clinician observation** | A clinician's dated observation in the clinician's own words | Clinician authorship, date, source context, and any later correction | Private scratch material unless deliberately retained or exported |
| **Model output** | Summary, possible question, formulation, contrast, metaphor, or relationship | Model identity, generation time, transformation, and every input source ID | Discarded at session end |
| **Clinician workspace** | Human-authored notes and working decisions, visibly separate from model text | Clinician author and timestamp; any AI edit appears as a redline for acceptance | Private scratchpad unless deliberately exported |
| **Retained working item** | A fact, artefact, preference, or practice instruction approved for later use | Originating item, author, purpose, audience, review date, expiry, and consent state where applicable | Item-level retention after explicit promotion |
| **Clinical record** | The practitioner's canonical record under the practice retention policy | Human-reviewed and authored for the record's purpose | Never written automatically by the cockpit |

Every card needs four independent facets. Do not collapse them into one confidence badge:

| Facet | Values | Question answered |
|---|---|---|
| **Origin** | client, clinician, external source, model | Who or what produced it? |
| **Transformation** | verbatim, summary, synthesis, hypothesis | What happened between source and display? |
| **Support** | source-linked, corroborated, conflicted, stale, unsupported, unknown | What does the available material support? |
| **Decision state** | unreviewed, pinned for session, shared, retained, exported, dismissed | What has a human decided to do with it? |

Directly recorded does not mean true. A clinician observation is not a client fact. A synthesis can also be conflicted, stale, or speculative. Avoid unexplained numeric confidence scores because they imply a calibration that has not been established.

Longitudinal memory must remain retrieval over sources, not a model-generated truth layer. [LongMemEval](https://proceedings.iclr.cc/paper_files/paper/2025/hash/d813d324dbf0598bbdc9c8e79740ed01-Abstract-Conference.html) found a 30% accuracy drop for commercial assistants and long-context models across sustained histories, with knowledge updates, temporal reasoning, and abstention among the tested abilities. In a clinical cockpit, a memory summary is an index to evidence, never the evidence itself.

The state transition should be deliberately narrow:

```text
approved source or clinician-entered cue
                    |
                    v
       ephemeral evidence and inference cards
                    |
          clinician chooses one action
                    |
       +------------+-------------------+
       |            |                   |
    discard    pin this session    propose retention
                                           |
                                   clinician review
                                           |
                         purpose, scope, review date, expiry
                                           |
                                  retained working item
```

There is no automatic path from source retrieval, raw session input, model inference, or clinician scratchpad into retained memory or the clinical record. "Pin for this session" and "retain for future sessions" are different actions. Deletion should work by item, category, session, or date, show dependent artefacts before the action, and read back what remains. A content-free audit event can preserve who did what and when without preserving deleted clinical content. Any future client-visible share is a separate product experiment, not a cockpit state transition.

### Solo feasibility

A synthetic cockpit prototype is highly feasible for one builder. Responsible live use is much harder because the value proposition depends on processing sensitive longitudinal material while the clinician is actively making care decisions.

Exploratory prototyping should not be narrowed to the eventual live regulatory boundary. With invented cases and simulated sessions, the prototype can freely test question suggestions, provisional formulations, inferred cycles, contradictory evidence, different memory designs, and cloud models. Governance becomes a promotion gate only when real-client health data or actual care enters the study.

The synthetic prototype should contain:

- invented cases and manually entered source cards;
- a slow workspace with timelines, episode links, contradictions, graphs, formulations, and questions;
- a simulated-session cue rail with one source-grounded cue at a time;
- both clinician-requested and system-offered cue conditions so their attentional cost can be compared;
- disposable model output and no clinical-record integration;
- explicit source-versus-inference labels;
- cloud and local model baselines measured on the same retrieval, latency, abstention, and provenance tasks.

### Falsifiable usability test

Create scripted simulated sessions with planted moments where a prior episode is relevant, moments where no cue is warranted, and plausible but misleading connections. Practitioner participants run the same cases in a counterbalanced comparison: no support, slow-workspace preparation only, clinician-requested cues, and system-offered cues.

Start with formative rounds to stabilise cue density, wording, dismissal, and provenance comprehension. Once the interaction stops changing materially, freeze the prototype and pre-register a confirmatory comparison. The cockpit passes only if it improves recognition of relevant prior material without a material loss in listening or judgment. Measure:

- proportion of planted opportunities correctly recognised;
- irrelevant or misleading cues shown and acted on;
- time from cue appearance to re-engagement with the simulated client;
- missed details on a post-session comprehension check;
- total cue glances, expansions, dismissals, and ignored cues;
- whether participants correctly distinguish source evidence from inference;
- simulated-client ratings of presence and interruption;
- clinician confidence in accepted and rejected cues, compared with correctness.

If recall improves but client detail is missed, false cues anchor the clinician, or participants cannot distinguish evidence from inference, the live rail has failed even if they say they like it. The slow workspace may still be useful on its own.

Provisional formulations and reinforcement graphs can be explored fully on synthetic cases. They are also the features most likely to move an eventual live intended purpose toward patient-specific clinical decision support, so real-client promotion needs a separate product-specific assessment and evidence plan.

## Primary hypothesis 2: the shared Session Canvas

The second hypothesis is also not a companion. It is a practitioner-operated, client-controlled canvas for creating one useful experience during a live session.

The canvas would have two views:

- A **practitioner console** for choosing the exercise, entering only the minimum context, previewing generated material, and stopping or discarding it.
- A **shared client surface** where the person can move, edit, reject, compare, and save only the material they want.

A shared artefact must preserve authorship: model-generated material, therapist framing, client edits, and any jointly agreed meaning remain visibly distinct. A client edit or jointly accepted metaphor does not silently become evidence about the client. Nothing flows back into private clinician memory or the clinical record without a separate decision for that purpose.

Use one mechanism per evaluable prototype, not a library. Several cheap synthetic prototypes can be compared before selecting a first product. Candidate mechanisms include:

- **Perspective map:** create several plausible accounts of the same event, then let the client rename, position, edit, or reject them.
- **Defusion studio:** turn a thought into a headline, character, image, weather system, or physical object that can be changed and placed at a chosen distance.
- **Values rehearsal:** build two or three short scenes showing different actions, then inspect the felt and practical tradeoffs rather than asking the model which is right.
- **Metaphor foundry:** co-create an image or story for an experience, with the client correcting what fits and what does not.

These are examples, not a product specification. The research programme should discover which single interaction repeatedly earns its place in a session.

### Why this can survive better general assistants

ChatGPT, Gemini, and other general systems can reproduce individual prompts. They are less naturally shaped around a therapist and client sharing control of one bounded experience. Oceanheart's possible advantage is therefore not model intelligence or permanent memory. It is the interaction grammar: practitioner authoring, client agency, explicit provenance, deliberate forgetting, and evidence from use in real practice.

That is not a permanent moat by itself. Large platforms can copy interfaces. The defensible part would have to grow from a tight learning loop with practitioners, trusted methods, measured failures, and a body of small interactions whose purpose and boundaries are clear.

### Commercial shape

Two tracks can reinforce each other without pretending that product-market fit already exists:

1. **Practice-led product discovery:** prototype and study one shared interaction with synthetic cases and practitioner peers.
2. **Consulting and evaluation:** help therapy and wellbeing organisations design, red-team, and evaluate AI interactions while the product thesis is still being tested.

Consulting may be the lowest-infrastructure route to early revenue because it uses the unusual combination already present in Oceanheart: long clinical practice, product engineering, AI evaluation, and fail-closed system design. That remains a commercial hypothesis. This scan contains no customer interviews, willingness-to-pay evidence, or sales test. The product track could provide original artefacts and evidence rather than generic advisory claims.

## Cockpit versus canvas

| Dimension | Private Clinician Cockpit | Shared Session Canvas |
|---|---|---|
| Primary user | Clinician | Clinician and client together |
| Core value | Retrieval, continuity, conceptual bandwidth, and alternative questions | Agency, externalisation, perspective change, and a shared artefact |
| Model influence | Hidden from the client unless the clinician makes it explicit | Visible and directly contestable by both people |
| Longitudinal data need | High if the full hypothesis is tested | Low for a single-session version |
| Closest visible competition | Upheal AI Assistant; Lyssn and Eleos in adjacent live-insight workflows | Early HCI prototypes and generic whiteboards, with fewer direct commercial anchors in this scan |
| Distinctiveness | Medium as a broad idea; potentially higher for a live visual renderer with strict provenance and disposable state | Higher as an interaction thesis, though individual exercises are easy to copy |
| Synthetic solo prototype | High | High |
| Live operational readiness | Not established until the exact data path and function are defined | Not established until the exact exercise, intended purpose, and data path are defined |
| Main evaluation question | Does it improve recall and reasoning without anchoring, distraction, or uncheckable influence? | Does it improve articulation or perspective without displacing the human relationship? |

Treat them as **independent synthetic hypotheses**. The cockpit may prove useful without a client surface; the canvas may prove useful without longitudinal memory. The current evidence does not establish that they should share a product architecture or that one must precede the other.

If both prove useful, a later Oceanheart Session Workbench could connect them through a hard boundary:

1. A private cockpit holds evidence, provisional inference, and clinician scratch work.
2. A deliberate `Share` action creates a new, versioned object on the client-visible canvas.
3. The client can edit or reject that shared object without gaining access to private hypotheses or notes.
4. Nothing returns to longitudinal memory or the clinical record without a separate, attended decision.

This is an integration option, not a current product decision. Building both together would muddle audiences, create accidental disclosure paths, and make it harder to discover which interaction has value. Kai's current primary research hypothesis is the cockpit, so test its slow workspace and cue rail first. Test the canvas separately if and when that question is active. Only then decide whether a deliberate handoff creates enough value to justify shared state.

## Recommended first move

Run a synthetic, practitioner-led discovery programme around situational recall:

1. Write 15 to 25 invented clinical moments in which a relevant prior episode, contradiction, question, or cycle is available in the case history but absent from working memory. Do not reconstruct identifiable client stories.
2. Turn those moments into a source-linked synthetic case set with dates, corrections, conflicts, irrelevant material, and facts that change over time.
3. Prototype the slow workspace first: timelines, connected episodes, alternative formulations, counterevidence, graphs, and candidate questions.
4. Derive a separate one-cue rail from it. Do not shrink the workspace UI. Design the rail around one line, one provenance state, one source action, and immediate dismissal.
5. Run the counterbalanced simulated-session test defined above. Compare no support, preparation only, requested cues, and system-offered cues.
6. Keep the slow workspace if it helps preparation. Promote the cue rail only if it improves relevant recall without degrading listening, comprehension, presence, or resistance to false salience.
7. Test the Shared Session Canvas separately if that hypothesis remains interesting. The human relationship stays unchanged by default.
8. Treat any real-client data or live-care trial as a new promotion decision behind the lawful, professional, security, and intended-purpose gates below.

The primary commercial hypothesis is: **Oceanheart helps clinicians recover the right source-linked thought at the moment of usefulness, without letting AI take over attention or authority.**

The complementary product hypothesis remains: **Oceanheart helps therapist and client turn one selected possibility into shared, inspectable material without turning the AI into the therapist.**

## Appendix A: promotion gate for real-client use in the UK

This section is a current research assessment, not a formal legal or regulatory classification of a finished product. It does not constrain synthetic or simulated opportunity exploration. It becomes load-bearing only when the work would process real-client health data, influence actual care, or make clinical product claims.

### Intended purpose governs

The [MHRA digital mental health guidance](https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/digital-mental-health-technology-qualification-and-classification) looks at the product's objective intended purpose across its interface, instructions, marketing, social posts, outputs, users, and clinical role. Calling something "wellbeing" or adding a "not medical advice" disclaimer does not cancel functions or claims aimed at diagnosis, prevention, treatment, clinical monitoring, or clinically significant symptoms. A therapist in the loop is not an automatic exemption if the software itself supplies patient-specific clinical information.

This supports a narrow first boundary: a therapist-controlled creative reflection aid that neither recommends treatment nor scores, diagnoses, predicts, triages, or monitors a condition. It does not prove that any particular implementation is outside medical-device regulation. The intended purpose and actual behaviour would still need a product-specific assessment before release.

The cockpit sits closer to the boundary. Reproducing an existing source for a clinician is different from summarising it, proposing a formulation, or generating information intended to influence treatment. The MHRA notes in its [current clinical-investigation guidance](https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/medical-devices-that-need-a-clinical-investigation/determining-if-a-clinical-investigations-is-required) that software merely displaying information is less likely to be a device, while summarisation may be one and software intended to influence treatment is more likely to be one. A private clinician display and retained human responsibility do not remove that question. Source retrieval, question prompts, formulations, and treatment suggestions should be specified as separate functions and assessed separately.

### Live therapy data raises the burden immediately

Therapy material and inferred psychological information will normally be special-category data. The ICO requires an [Article 6 lawful basis and a separate Article 9 condition](https://ico.org.uk/for-organisations/uk-gdpr-guidance-and-resources/lawful-basis/special-category-data/what-are-the-rules-on-special-category-data/). Explicit consent can sometimes be the Article 9 condition, but it is not a universal answer and must be specific, informed, freely given, granular, and withdrawable. An equivalent no-AI route matters in a therapeutic relationship.

Four questions must remain separate:

1. Does the client agree to the therapeutic activity?
2. Does the client agree to this optional use of AI and understand its influence?
3. What permits the practitioner to disclose confidential material to each processor?
4. What Article 6 basis and Article 9 condition make each processing purpose lawful?

The [ICO says healthcare consent is often not the appropriate UK GDPR lawful basis](https://ico.org.uk/for-organisations/uk-gdpr-guidance-and-resources/lawful-basis/consent/when-is-consent-appropriate/), even where a practitioner separately needs agreement to treatment or data sharing. Article 9(2)(h) can apply to health or social care under the required professional confidentiality. At Article 6, contract is available only where the AI processing is objectively necessary to deliver the contracted service; the [ICO's AI lawfulness guidance](https://ico.org.uk/for-organisations/uk-gdpr-guidance-and-resources/artificial-intelligence/guidance-on-ai-and-data-protection/how-do-we-ensure-lawfulness-in-ai/) says it cannot be used where a less intrusive route provides the same service. Legitimate interests may be a candidate Article 6 basis after a documented necessity and balancing test. Explicit consent may instead be considered for one or both layers. The right combination depends on the controller, service, population, purpose, and ability to offer therapy without AI. This report does not select it.

Withdrawal also has layers. The client must be able to stop future AI use without losing the ordinary therapy route. If UK GDPR consent is the lawful basis, withdrawal has its legal consequences for that processing. Required clinical records may have a different lawful basis and retention duty. A cockpit must therefore distinguish deletion of optional working memory, deletion at cloud processors, and retention of the approved clinical record. It must never promise that one button erases all three unless readback proves it.

A live use would also require a data-flow map, purpose and retention decisions, processor contracts and subprocessor review, international-transfer analysis where relevant, an incident route, and a DPIA. The [ICO identifies sensitive data, vulnerable people, evaluation or scoring, systematic monitoring, and innovative technology](https://ico.org.uk/for-organisations/uk-gdpr-guidance-and-resources/accountability-and-governance/data-protection-impact-assessments-dpias/when-do-we-need-to-do-a-dpia/) as high-risk indicators. AI used in therapy can combine several at once.

Cloud inference does not make the design unacceptable, and local inference does not make it safe. The cloud route needs the exact model endpoint, data region, logging and abuse-monitoring behaviour, retention, backup and deletion semantics, subprocessors, transfer mechanism, access controls, incident route, and signed processor terms verified. "Not used for training" covers only one purpose. Data minimisation should send only the source fragments needed for the current invocation, not the entire case history by default.

### The professional boundary is separate

The current [BACP Ethical Framework](https://www.bacp.co.uk/events-and-resources/ethics-and-standards/ethical-framework-for-the-counselling-professions/?ContensisTextOnly=true) requires competence, adequate insurance, appropriate records, and clear confidentiality practice. Its [new framework](https://www.bacp.co.uk/media/23628/ethical-framework-for-the-counselling-professions-2025.pdf), mandatory from 3 November 2026, expressly addresses risk assessment, competence, storage, confidentiality, and honesty when using AI and digital tools.

Before any live-client pilot, the practitioner would need to confirm competence, supervision, and indemnity cover for the exact activity. The client needs a clear account of the AI's purpose, data use, limits, benefits, risks, whether outputs are visible only to the clinician, and how those outputs may shape the conversation. Recording or transcription requires a separate agreement and materially increases risk. The conservative first live experiment therefore uses no recording, no raw transcript, no persistent personal memory, no automatic clinical record, no minors, and no crisis promise.

### Minimum governance pack before any live-client use

- Fixed intended purpose and prohibited uses.
- Adult population and explicit exclusions.
- Data-flow map, Article 6 basis, Article 9 condition, DPIA, processor and transfer review.
- Client information, granular choices, and an equivalent no-AI route.
- Synthetic red-team cases covering trauma, suicidality, psychosis, mania, eating disorders, culture, and disability.
- Therapist preview of every output, with stop and discard controls.
- Incident route, retention policy, insurer response, supervision discussion, and written stop criteria.
- No reuse of live therapy material for prompt development, model training, or product evaluation.

## Appendix C: scope for the next pass

The broad market scan should remain a dated reference, but the next research slice can focus on Upheal, Blueprint, Mentalyc, NovoPsych, Aetios, Lyssn, and Eleos as direct or adjacent cockpit comparators. Do not add a larger startup directory: it would create false completeness without improving the decision.

Do not cut questions, formulations, inferred cycles, or alternative memory designs from synthetic exploration merely because they would raise a later live-use burden. The point of the synthetic prototype is to discover which capability has value.

Cut these from any first real-client promotion: ambient listening, full transcripts, automatic memory extraction, hidden clinical-record writes, diagnostic or risk outputs, minors, crisis promises, and any cloud path whose processor, retention, deletion, transfer, or security behaviour has not been verified.
