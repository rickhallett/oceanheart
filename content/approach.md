I do not begin with a technique or a tool. I begin with the situation as it actually runs: who is present, what repeats, what is avoided, where the handoffs are, and what happens when something goes wrong.

The response may be a conversation, a boundary, a change to the surrounding environment, or a piece of software. The sequence below stays consistent.

## Enter the real context

For systems work, this means walking through the current process with the person doing it. We identify the trigger, decisions, exceptions, owner, and present cost.

For relational work, it means attending to what is happening between us instead of replacing it with an account of what ought to be happening. In an encounter with AI, it means watching both the model and the person meeting it.

Descriptions are useful. The live situation is better evidence.

## Find the live constraint

A constraint may sit in software, a role, an assumption, a missing boundary, or the design of the surrounding environment. Naming the wrong one produces a polished answer to the wrong problem.

I stay with the situation until we can say what is holding, what activates it, what it costs, and who is carrying it.

## Choose the smallest useful response

Sometimes the answer is a simpler process. Sometimes it is lightweight automation, custom software, AI assistance, a direct conversation, or a clearer limit. Complexity has to earn its place.

When AI is involved, I use models for work such as classification, drafting, retrieval, or proposing a next move. Plausible output does not become truth because a model produced it. Where consequences matter, deterministic rules and an accountable person decide what reaches somebody else.

## Build for failure as well as success

A prototype has to earn its place in real use. Before building, I define the baseline, the owner, the safety boundary, and what evidence would count as an improvement.

Failed work needs somewhere to go. Errors should be visible, approval should be explicit, important decisions should leave a trace, and a person should be able to recover the situation without reconstructing it from memory.

## Stay close enough to see

I ship into the place where the work happens, observe what changes, and follow up. If it is a system, I document its operation and recovery path. If it is relational work, we name what the frame includes and how it ends.

The handover matters. The person responsible for the work should be able to carry it without depending on me.

## Keep the human boundary explicit

Fifteen years as a therapist taught me to design deliberately around vulnerability, consent, trust, and the moment when responsibility must stay with a person. Administrative work can disappear. Clinical judgement cannot.

The same principle applies beyond clinical settings: consequential decisions need an identifiable owner, and convenience does not outrank the person affected by the system.

## Tools and evidence

I work across TypeScript, Python, web applications, APIs, databases, payments, cloud infrastructure, evaluation systems, and frontier-model APIs. The tools change. The useful part is tracing what happened across the whole situation, explaining it clearly, and repairing the right boundary.
